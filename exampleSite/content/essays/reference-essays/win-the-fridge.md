Win the fridge is the practice of using every scrap of food before it goes to waste: working through the fridge, freezer, and pantry regularly instead of letting things quietly expire in the back. It's a frugal habit and an ethical one at the same time: food waste is avoidable, and being well-fed enough to have a fridge full of choices worth wasting is a privilege, not a given.

It's the same idea nose-to-tail butchery and root-to-stem cooking are built on, just applied to an ordinary week instead of a whole animal or a single vegetable: every cut, every stalk and leaf and peel, everything that would otherwise go in the bin but is still delicious and nourishing, gets used. A carrot top is a garnish. Onion skins and a chicken carcass are stock, not garbage. The heel of a block of parmesan flavours a pot of soup long after the last shaving is gone.

## Why it's an engineering problem, not a willpower problem

I used to treat "don't waste food" as a discipline I kept failing at: a vague virtue I'd resolve to do better on, then forget about until something furry turned up behind the milk. What actually worked was reframing it as a systems problem instead of a moral one. A fridge doesn't lose a war of willpower; it loses because nothing was planned to use what's already in it.

Two habits do most of the work:

- **Shop against what's already there.** Before planning a week of meals, look at what's already open, cut, or about to turn; that's the starting ingredient list, not an afterthought bolted onto a shopping list built from scratch.
- **Build a step that absorbs scraps.** A soup, a stir-fry, a stock, a frittata: something in the week's rotation that's genuinely improved by throwing in "whatever's around," not just tolerant of it. [[Lazy tomato soup]] is built exactly this way: the base recipe is fixed, but the last handful of vegetables in the method is a deliberate gap for whatever needs using. Pasta sauces work the same way: half a container of sauce from the freezer, or the last of the cherry tomatoes and a few cloves of garlic, becomes dinner in the time the pasta takes.

Both are things you set up once, not things you have to remember to do every single day. That's the engineering move: the unglamorous fix isn't more discipline, it's a system that makes the wasteful path the harder one.

## The takeaway

Winning the fridge isn't a single virtuous cooking session where you use up everything at once; it's closer to a maintenance habit, the kitchen equivalent of clearing your inbox a little every day instead of once a year. Recipes tagged win-the-fridge across this cookbook share the same shape: a fixed technique with a deliberate, flexible slot for whatever's about to turn. Start there if you're looking for a place to practice.
